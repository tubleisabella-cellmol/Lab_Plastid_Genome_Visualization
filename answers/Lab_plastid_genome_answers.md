
## 1. Scientific name and accession
*Hibiscus syriacus* L., NCBI RefSeq accession NC_026909.1.

## 2. Total length
161,019 bp. This is printed in the centre of the OGDRAW map and matches the Galaxy result from the previous lab.

## 3. LSC, SSC, IRa and IRb on the map
Yes. The map is circular. The LSC is the long arc across the top, and the SSC is the short arc at the bottom. The two inverted repeats are the thick black arcs on the left and right sides of the ring that separate the LSC from the SSC (OGDRAW labels both as "IR" on the GC graph). From the genes they contain, the left IR (IRb) holds *ycf2*, and the right IR (IRa) holds *rpl2*, *rpl23*, *ndhB* and *rps7*. Region sizes are not shown on the map; I took them from the genome report (LSC 89,698 bp, SSC 19,831 bp, IR 25,745 bp each).

## 4. Three genes in the LSC
*psbA*, *rbcL* and *rpoB* (others include *psaA*, *psaB*, *atpA* and *petA*).

## 5. Gene in the SSC
*ndhA* (others include *ndhH*, *ndhD*, *psaC* and *rps15*).

## 6. Gene in an inverted repeat
*ycf2* lies in the left IR, and *ndhB*, *rps7*, *rpl2* and *rpl23* lie in the right IR. Because the IR is duplicated, each of these genes should exist in two copies. In this record only one copy of each is annotated, so the map shows each of them once. The exception is *rps12*, which appears in both IRs (and once in the LSC) because its trans-spliced exons are annotated in each copy. In a fully annotated plastome, IR genes would be drawn twice.

## 7. A photosynthesis gene
*psbA* encodes the D1 protein, a core subunit of the photosystem II reaction center that takes part in light-driven electron transfer and water splitting.

## 8. Direction of gene arrows
The grey arrows on the map show that genes drawn outside the ring are transcribed counterclockwise and genes drawn inside the ring are transcribed clockwise. For example, *rbcL*, *psbC* and *psbD* are drawn outside, and *psaA*, *psaB*, *psbA* and *rpoB* are drawn inside. So genes are encoded on both DNA strands, and transcription does not run in a single direction around the genome. Neighbouring genes in the same orientation (for example *rpoB*, *rpoC1* and *rpoC2*) may be transcribed together, but the map alone does not prove this.

## 10. Why a map is useful
A map shows the whole genome at once, so the LSC, SSC and IR structure, the order and orientation of genes, and clusters of related genes (for example the *ndh* genes in the SSC or the *rpo* genes in the LSC) are visible immediately. A raw DNA sequence or a long annotation list does not show this. The map also reveals annotation problems: in this record no rRNA genes are drawn, most IR genes appear only once, and I could not find *accD*, *cemA* or *ccsA*.
